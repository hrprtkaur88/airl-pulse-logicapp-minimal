## Core thought-leadership argument

Generative AI is reshaping how people live, work, and think. Organizations are at different stages of adoption, but the leadership question is consistent: whether leaders can help teams adopt new ways of working, redefine success, and navigate uncertainty.

Korn Ferry frames leaders as the bridge between unrealized AI value and well-orchestrated impact. AI’s value is realized when human and artificial intelligence are brought together to amplify what can be achieved.

The report narrative should therefore position the assessment pattern as a leadership signal, not a technology signal. The output should help client teams understand whether current leaders are ready to guide people, prioritize effort, redesign work, address fear, and scale value.

## Key Human + AI framing

Use these ideas to strengthen report narrative:

- AI leadership is every leader’s responsibility, not just the responsibility of technical experts.
- Leaders need to do more than encourage safe experimentation. They need to reimagine processes, systems, and work itself.
- AI should be positioned as a collaborator that elevates human potential, not as a replacement narrative.
- Leadership matters most when organizations are moving through ambiguity, adoption anxiety, changing roles, and unclear value pathways.
- AI-ready leaders guide teams through ambiguity, create clarity where little exists, and support employees who may fear for their jobs.
- The Human + AI era requires skills, structures, and systems to evolve quickly.
- The leadership challenge is to match AI capability with the right people, roles, capabilities, and human performance conditions.

## The six responsibilities

### 1. Sustain the Vision

Responsibility meaning:
AI-Ready Leaders anchor teams around a compelling AI vision and maintain focus through challenges and the constant evolution of AI capability. They keep pointing the way even when current efforts do not immediately yield results.

Narrative use:
Use this when interpreting whether leaders can hold the AI ambition steady, create confidence in a future state, and prevent momentum from fading when the value path is uncertain.

When high:
Strong results may signal that leaders can keep people oriented to a credible AI-enabled future and maintain commitment through ambiguity.

When lower:
Lower results may suggest that teams could lose focus when AI initiatives take longer to mature, business priorities shift, or the connection between experimentation and value is unclear.

### 2. Take Decisive Action

Responsibility meaning:
AI-Ready Leaders act with deliberate, not reckless, urgency. They provide clarity, align teams, and balance when to perfect, progress, pause, or stop.

Narrative use:
Use this when interpreting whether leaders can move AI from discussion into decisions, ownership, and operating rhythm.

When high:
Strong results may signal decision energy, urgency, and team alignment. This can help the client move priority AI use cases out of concept and into execution.

When lower:
Lower results may suggest risk of delay, unclear ownership, excessive caution, or fragmented experimentation without decisive movement.

### 3. Scale for Impact

Responsibility meaning:
AI-Ready Leaders prioritize efforts that create the largest impact and most meaningful outcomes. They move away from fragmented experiments and focus on AI efforts that promise the most value.

Narrative use:
Use this when interpreting whether leaders can convert pilots into repeatable enterprise value.

When high:
Strong results may signal prioritization discipline and the ability to concentrate resources on the few AI bets that matter most.

When lower:
Lower results may suggest risk of innovation theatre, scattered pilots, limited accountability, or difficulty converting AI activity into measurable value.

### 4. Don’t Stop at Success

Responsibility meaning:
AI-Ready Leaders avoid complacency. What works today may quickly become obsolete, so they continue experimenting, iterating, creating, and exploring alternative Human + AI systems.

Narrative use:
Use this when interpreting whether leaders will keep improving after early wins and prevent pilots from becoming isolated or prematurely locked in.

When high:
Strong results may signal curiosity, iteration, and willingness to challenge first answers as AI capability and work requirements evolve.

When lower:
Lower results may suggest risk that early wins become endpoints, teams stop learning too soon, or the organization fails to refresh AI-enabled workflows as the technology changes.

### 5. Champion Learning and Unlearning

Responsibility meaning:
AI-Ready Leaders spark rapid learning and unlearning. They encourage AI fluency, curiosity, workflow redesign, and deliberate rethinking of long-held habits.

Narrative use:
Use this when interpreting whether leaders can help teams refresh skills, let go of old work patterns, and redesign routines for Human + AI work.

When high:
Strong results may signal adaptability and the capacity to help teams learn through discomfort.

When lower:
Lower results may suggest risk that AI remains an add-on tool rather than a catalyst for redesigning skills, roles, habits, and workflows.

### 6. Address Fears

Responsibility meaning:
AI-Ready Leaders address fears around AI’s impact on jobs and career paths with integrity, authenticity, visibility, and trustworthiness.

Narrative use:
Use this when interpreting whether leaders can create the trust conditions needed for AI adoption.

When high:
Strong results may signal the ability to stabilize people through uncertainty, discuss difficult implications directly, and reduce hidden resistance.

When lower:
Lower results may suggest that fear, skepticism, or job anxiety could constrain adoption even if the technical strategy is sound.

## Recommended report language patterns

Use language like:

- leadership readiness as the bridge between AI ambition and orchestrated impact
- Human + AI transformation requires leaders to guide people through ambiguity and redesign work
- the assessment pattern shows where leaders may be ready to convert AI potential into adoption, trust, and scaled value
- the issue is not AI awareness alone; it is whether leaders can create the conditions for AI-enabled work to take hold
- strong results should be treated as deployment assets; lower results should be treated as enablement signals

Avoid generic language like:

- AI readiness is important for the future
- leaders need to adopt AI
- this score shows strengths and weaknesses
- development is needed in lower areas

## Guardrails

- Do not imply the assessment proves the client’s AI strategy will succeed or fail.
- Do not imply the sample represents the full organization.
- Do not overstate gaps as statistically significant.
- Do not call the report a dedicated AI-Ready Leader diagnostic.
- Keep the language directional, client-ready, and commercially useful.
